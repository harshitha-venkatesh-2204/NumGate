Executives increasingly read weekly notes that large language models (LLMs) draft from sales tables, where one wrong number can mislead a decision. We present NumGate, an open harness that checks every number in such a note against its source table with a two-level rule-based gate (is the number in the table or derivable from it, and is it attached to the right entity, metric and period), and compares four generation strategies on 60 weekly tables built from the public UCI Online Retail II data. In a pre-registered study of 1,440 notes from three Claude models (Opus 5, Sonnet 5 and Haiku 4.5), every number the gate flagged on the 54 tables not used in the pilot, and a random sample of unflagged numbers, was reviewed blind by AI agents, not people. Confirmed errors are rare: 0.22% of checked numbers for Opus 5, 0.05% for Sonnet 5 and 0.47% for Haiku 4.5. These are lower bounds, because only flagged numbers can be confirmed, and the sample of unflagged numbers cannot exclude true rates of up to about 2.44% to 2.92%. The main result concerns measurement. Only 30.6% of the gate's flags were real errors, and its precision depended on the writer, from 43.1% for Haiku 4.5 to 7.6% for Sonnet 5, where one phrasing that the gate misreads, a gap held-out testing had already found, caused most false alarms. Raw gate rates cannot tell Sonnet 5 from Opus 5. Compute-then-write raised Opus 5's error rate through rounding and mislabelled directions in its own facts script. Write-then-verify and a facts template reduced Haiku 4.5's errors under a conditional test and a sign test, although the pre-registered test no longer separates them once rounding slips are set aside; for Opus 5 their reductions rest on six rounding slips and are not robust. Faithfulness rates from automatic checkers should be reported with the checker's precision measured on the same text.
